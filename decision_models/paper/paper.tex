\section{Introduction}
Financial document triage routes incoming information to automatic skipping or
analyst review. Its value depends on how much reading it removes, how much
important information it misses, and its processing cost.

We classify public financial documents as \emph{routine} or \emph{review-worthy}
using their content alone. Potentially significant but unresolved developments
go to review; uncertainty is metadata, not a third destination. Labels supplied
by a language-model teacher define this review convention. They do not establish
investment materiality, which can depend on company history and portfolio context.

We ask whether adapting a pretrained decision model improves triage relative to
a finance encoder and a lexical classifier, and whether any gain warrants its
processing cost. Seven systems form the core comparison: native Laya, Laya with
head adaptation or low-rank adaptation (LoRA), frozen FinBERT with logistic
regression, FinBERT with LoRA, word TF-IDF with logistic regression, and a majority
baseline. Trainable core systems share 4,000 documents; larger nested subsets
measure sample efficiency.

The contribution is a financial-domain comparison with event-group controls,
matched pre-update models, and reproducible error, workload and resource
measurements. The binary study follows a three-class exploration and reuses its
test documents. We froze binary choices before reevaluation, but prior inspection
limits the independence of the resulting evidence.

\section{Related work}
Financial relevance filtering is established. Ahbali et al.\ \citep{credit}
use a TF-IDF/SVM relevance filter before corporate credit-event analysis.
Del Corro and Hoffart \citep{financialrelevance} derive financial-news relevance
from stock-prediction attention. FinLin \citep{finlin} provides multi-source,
expert-annotated entity relevance and sentiment. These targets differ from
whole-document source-only review triage.

Closely related decision-model studies also exist. Nandakishore
\citep{assessor} evaluates Laya Typed-Decisions, teacher agreement, calibration
and selective escalation on synthetic workflows. Stuani's engineering
benchmark \citep{complaints} compares Laya, lexical classifiers, encoders and
language models on financial complaint routing and fraud flags. Borchers
\citep{borchers} compares adapted decision models with frozen representations
and learning curves on research-paper categories. Our task and corpus differ;
we do not claim to introduce financial filtering, decision-model comparison
or calibration analysis.

Laya's English root checkpoint uses ModernBERT-large and a learned typed-decision
head \citep{laya,modernbert}. FinBERT's adaptation to financial text and sentiment
\citep{finbert} makes it a relevant industry comparator, but its sentiment head
does not supply review labels. We use its encoder with a task classifier.
LoRA \citep{lora} and temperature scaling \citep{guo} are established methods.
Automated annotation still requires task-specific validation \citep{annotation};
success on another annotation task does not validate this rubric.

\section{Task and data}
\label{sec:data}
The input is an extracted English document body. Ordinary administrative content
is routine; a potentially significant corporate, financial, economic or policy
development warrants review. Scheduled publication and sentiment alone do not
determine the label. Source identifiers and issuer history are not additional
features; metadata support acquisition, grouping and evaluation.

The corpus comprises SEC filings and release exhibits, Federal Reserve releases,
ECB institutional/statistical releases, GOV.UK announcements and Wikinews.
Training uses 25,000 documents dated through March 2026; selection and calibration
use 1,000 and 500 documents from April--June; test uses 2,000 from July--September.
Related-document groups cannot cross cohorts. Table~\ref{tab:data} shows the
substantial SEC dominance. The small news sample does not represent a general
financial-news inbox.

We retain earlier definite labels and use Luna Flex with a pinned binary rubric
to decide previously unclear references and label new documents. Unclear labels
are therefore not mechanically merged into either class. A blinded, balanced
200-document training-only audit checks consistency of retained references
against new binary decisions without overriding them. This hybrid protocol
measures teacher consistency, not agreement with financial analysts.

Evaluation additions were chosen without labels or model scores while retaining
previous memberships. The revised test keeps 1,800 earlier bodies and adds 200.
Grouping excludes exact matches, known event aliases and verified near-duplicates;
approximate retrieval and inherited groups can miss relationships. Acquisition
and extraction details are retained in the public evidence package.

\section{Systems and evaluation}
\label{sec:methods}
\subsection{Fixed comparisons and adaptation}
Training subsets of 4,000, 16,000 and 25,000 documents are nested, with stable
round-robin family/class sampling. All trainable core systems use the same
4,000 bodies; the majority reference counts the same labels without optimization. Native Laya
requires no task training. TF-IDF, frozen FinBERT/logistic and both LoRA systems
additionally use 16,000 and 25,000 training documents. Every learning-curve point also
uses the same 1,500 development labels; training count is not total annotation cost.

TF-IDF learns its vocabulary and transforms from training alone. Logistic
regularization and class weighting use the same bounded grid at each training
size, selected by selection macro F1 (Appendix~\ref{sec:supporting}).
Frozen FinBERT windows are averaged and L2-normalized before classification.
Caches bind body, recipe and source identities; command time includes encoding
and classifier search.

We use pinned \texttt{convaiinnovations/laya} English root and
\texttt{ProsusAI/finbert} checkpoints; full revisions accompany the evidence.
Adapted neural systems use seed 42, unweighted document-level cross-entropy and
up to three epochs. Raw selection macro F1 chooses the checkpoint; epoch zero
is eligible, and ties retain the earlier epoch. Laya head adaptation updates
the decision head with a frozen encoder at learning rate $3\times10^{-5}$.
LoRA updates rank-eight encoder adapters and the task head at $10^{-4}$, with
scaling 16 and adapter dropout 0.05. Matched pre-update states are preserved
as supporting controls, avoiding a direct attribution of every SDK-to-adapted
change to weight updates.

Laya adaptation uses a 1,024-token context with overlapping windows and averages
their logits. FinBERT uses a 512-token context with non-overlapping source windows
and averages their embeddings before classification. Both retain all normalized
source tokens and use one document-level loss; no window labels are inferred.
The native Laya SDK uses 512-token windows and limits GPU passes to eight windows,
retaining its most-confident long-document aggregation. It therefore differs from
adapted Laya in context and pooling as well as fitting. Architectures, parameter counts,
pretraining objectives and classifier recipes differ across systems; those
comparisons cannot isolate finance pretraining or LoRA as a sole cause.

\subsection{Agreement, misses and review workload}
Macro F1 averages the routine and review-worthy class F1 values. Exact binary
ties route to review. We also report review precision/recall, the $2\times2$
confusion matrix, and false negatives divided by review-worthy reference
support. At the default decision, a document predicted routine is skipped.
The skipped fraction quantifies reading removed; its complement is review
workload. A low miss rate can reflect more review rather than better
discrimination, so both quantities accompany every operational comparison.

Scalar temperature is fit by calibration negative log likelihood. It changes
confidence while preserving the default class decision. Binary Brier score is
the sum over both class columns, equal to twice the positive-class squared
error. We report raw/calibrated scoring arithmetic in the evidence package.

We fit five routine-only skip policies on calibration: empirical review-miss
targets of 1\% and 5\%, and maximum skip budgets of 10\%, 20\% and 30\%.
Each chooses the largest feasible calibration skip count without splitting
equal-probability ties. Predicted review documents are never skipped by these
rules. We report achieved test skip counts and missed references, rather than
assume workloads match or that fitting targets are population guarantees.

The fixed primary comparison is 4k Laya LoRA versus 4k word TF-IDF/logistic.
We use 2,000 paired whole-group bootstrap draws for percentile 95\% intervals
and their differences. These intervals condition on a single fitted run;
they do not measure training instability or correct supplementary comparisons
for multiplicity. Source/length slices are descriptive, with support reported.
The original 16-comparison freeze preceded revised-cohort evaluation. The two
25k LoRA fits were added after those results, using the inherited fixed recipes;
their selected checkpoints and calibration were fixed before scoring them on
the same test cohort.

\subsection{Measured resources}
Serial GPU jobs use an NVIDIA RTX 3060 (12 GiB); CPU work uses an Intel
Core i5-12400F. Warm inference includes document processing but excludes loading,
using the same 128 selection bodies. We report mean/P95 latency, throughput,
allocated GPU memory and fitting/command durations. Appendix~\ref{sec:supporting}
accounts for preparation and interrupted attempts. Timings describe this machine.

\section{Discussion and limitations}
\input{generated/discussion.tex}

Teacher agreement is the main limitation. Training and evaluation share a teacher,
and retained and newly decided labels follow different protocols. Expert
adjudication is needed to relate classification misses to economically material
information. The study supplies neither company/portfolio context nor evidence
about trading profitability.

A temporally later but previously inspected test cannot independently confirm
generalization. Source imbalance, changing web content, possible pretraining
exposure and missed duplicates further limit transfer to industry inboxes.

Fixed recipes and one seed leave optimization variability and the best achievable
performance within each model family unresolved. Supplementary intervals are
uncorrected for multiple comparisons, and routing thresholds may shift with
source mix. Frontier generative classifiers, full-model adaptation and RLCD
training are outside this comparison.
